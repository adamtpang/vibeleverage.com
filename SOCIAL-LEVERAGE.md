# Social leverage: the fifth lever

One sentence: social capital is leverage because one relationship can multiply output many times over at near-zero marginal cost once it exists, but unlike code and media it needs another person's yes, so it follows media instead of leading it.

Written 2026-09-18 from a PangPod session. Adam's goal that prompted it: eventually meet, befriend and help David Senra, using PangPod as the social capital growth strategy.

## Why it is leverage

Naval's four levers (code, media, capital, labor) all multiply one person's output. Relationships do too: one introduction, collaboration or recommendation can do what a year of solo work cannot. The difference is permission. Code and media are permissionless. Social capital needs the other person to say yes, which is why MASTERPLAN.md's order still holds: ship media first, and let the media earn the relationships.

## How to measure it

| Measure | What it counts | Research behind it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Degree | How many people you know | Basic network analysis |
| Brokerage | How many separate groups you connect that do not know each other | Ronald Burt, structural holes: brokers see more ideas and get rewarded for it |
| Eigenvector / PageRank | Whether the people you know are themselves well connected | The math behind Google's link ranking |
| Tie strength | How deep each relationship is | Mark Granovetter: weak ties bring most new information and job leads |
| Reciprocity | Whether help flows both ways over time | Adam Grant's giver research |

Brokerage matters most. A person who bridges groups that do not talk to each other is worth more to each group than someone who knows many people inside one crowd.

## What the best-documented high scorers teach

**Paul Erdős, mathematician.** Roughly 500 co-authors, so many that mathematicians measure their distance from him as an "Erdős number." He showed up in person and worked on the other person's problem.
Lesson: bring your full attention to their work, not a request for help with yours.

**Cosimo de' Medici, Florence.** Padgett and Ansell's 1993 network study ("Robust Action and the Rise of the Medici") found the Medici rose by marrying and trading into families that were not connected to each other, so every path ran through them.
Lesson: be the bridge between worlds that do not talk. Brokerage beats headcount.

**Benjamin Franklin, Philadelphia.** In 1727 he formed the Junto, a small weekly club of tradesmen for mutual improvement. It went on to seed a lending library, a volunteer fire company and what became the University of Pennsylvania. His autobiography also describes winning over a rival by asking to borrow a rare book, now called the Ben Franklin effect.
Lesson: a small recurring group that makes things together compounds. Asking a small favor can build a tie faster than doing one.

**Paul Revere, Boston.** David Hackett Fischer's history of the midnight ride shows Revere belonged to more of Boston's revolutionary groups than almost anyone, which is why his warning spread and others did not.
Lesson: belong to many small groups and show up in person. Reach comes from membership across groups, not status inside one.

**Tim Ferriss, podcasting.** His first episode featured his friend and co-host Kevin Rose, and the show then became a way to spend hours with people he wanted to learn from.
Lesson: an interview is a legitimate reason to spend two hours with anyone. The podcast is the relationship engine.

## The pattern across all five

1. **Give first.** Every one of them was useful before they asked for anything.
2. **Bridge, do not cluster.** Their value came from connecting separate worlds.
3. **Make something together.** Papers, marriages and trade, libraries, a revolution, episodes. Shared work turns acquaintances into ties.
4. **Repeat in person.** Recurring contact, not one-off introductions.
